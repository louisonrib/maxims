# Shoulders of giants

> "If I have seen further it is by standing on the shoulders of Giants."
> — Isaac Newton, letter to Robert Hooke, 5 February 1675/6

Newton took the image from Bernard of Chartres, five centuries before him.

Left to yourself, you start from a blank page: you generate the plausible
version of the thing from memory, and it comes out as the average of
everything you have seen, without the decisions the best practitioners
already made or the reasons behind them. Someone has almost always faced
this problem before, often with more time, more users, and the scars to show
for it. Their work is the strongest material you can start from, and
concrete examples are what you learn from best. Look first, then make.

## Steps

1. **Name the thing and its family.** One sentence: what you are about to
   make or decide. Then who else has made it: direct competitors and
   comparable products, the reference projects of the field, its standard or
   canonical practice, and the neighbouring field that solved it under
   another name. Write down the words each of them would use: one idea goes
   by different names across communities, and a search in one vocabulary
   finds only that community.

2. **Look at home first.** The nearest giants are already here: this
   project's own conventions and earlier work, what the platform, framework,
   or library does by itself, the tools and skills already installed, the
   user's notes and past decisions. What exists here is the frame everything
   else must fit.

3. **Study the works themselves.** Pick a few strong examples — the
   best-regarded, the most used, the one closest to your constraints — and
   open the actual artifact: the product screen or help page, the
   repository, the published document, the spec. A ranked list or a summary
   tells you what exists; the work shows how it was done. Done when you can
   say, for each example, what it does on the point that matters to you, with
   a link.

4. **Read the landscape.** Where the examples converge, you have the
   convention: what people already expect, and your default. Where they
   diverge, you have the real design choices, each with someone who made it.
   What none of them does, or what only your situation requires, is where
   your own contribution lies.

5. **Report before you build.** Before the first draft, give the user a few
   lines: what you looked at (linked), what you take, what you leave and why.
   At this point changing course costs a reply, not a rewrite. When no one is
   waiting to reply, put those lines at the top of the deliverable.

6. **Build on it, then go further.** Start from the convention and diverge
   where your constraints give you a reason, naming the reason. Borrow
   structure, patterns, and decisions freely; reuse code and text as far as
   their licenses allow, and credit them. The examples are a floor: Newton's
   point was to see further than the giants, not to stand still on them.

## Proportion

The look scales with what is at stake and how new the thing is. A one-line
fix, a choice the codebase or the user already dictates, or ground you
surveyed earlier in this session needs no survey. A new screen, module,
process, or document format earns a few minutes of looking; a decision that
shapes a product or much future work earns more. When nothing comparable
turns up, say where you looked and under which words, then build from first
principles.
